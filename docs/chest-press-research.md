# Chest Press Research Note

Last reviewed: 2026-06-25

## Current movement classification

Movement: Seated / machine chest press, treated as a horizontal press pattern.

Primary:
- Pectoralis major

Secondary:
- Anterior deltoid
- Triceps brachii

Support:
- Serratus anterior
- Rotator cuff

## Why this classification

The best direct evidence for this exact family of movements is a surface EMG comparison of horizontal bench press and seated chest press variations. It measured the clavicular, sternal, and costal portions of pectoralis major, anterior deltoid, and triceps brachii during seated chest press grips and bench press grips at 60% 1RM. It found broadly similar activation across the horizontal press variants, with pectoralis major portions and anterior deltoid prominent, and triceps brachii changing more with grip width.

The broader bench-press EMG literature supports pectoralis major and triceps brachii as dominant movers in horizontal pressing, with anterior deltoid generally lower than pectoralis major/triceps in flat barbell bench press. Because a seated machine constrains the path and often reduces stabilization demand, we should not automatically transfer every free-weight bench press support finding into the machine version, but the movement family is still informative.

Rotator cuff is included as support because bench press research measuring shoulder muscles reports direction-specific rotator cuff recruitment during bench press tasks, especially posterior cuff activity such as infraspinatus, consistent with dynamic shoulder stabilization.

Serratus anterior is included as support because pressing requires scapular control/protraction, and chest press/bench press comparison research reports serratus anterior activity with scapular kinematic assessment. It should not be treated as a primary chest-press hypertrophy target from the current evidence.

## Important caveats

- EMG is not a direct measure of hypertrophy, mechanical tension, or long-term adaptation.
- Surface EMG is vulnerable to electrode placement, crosstalk, normalization choices, load, tempo, fatigue, and technique.
- Chest press machine geometry varies by machine. Handle path, seat height, grip angle, back pad angle, and elbow path can change muscle involvement.
- The current app classification is a practical target-map, not a claim that these are the only active muscles.

## Rating review

Scores are 0-100, where 100 is best.

- Ease: 86. Machine chest press is relatively easy to learn because the user is seated, braced, and guided through a fixed or semi-fixed path. It is high on the global scale compared with free-weight compounds, Olympic lifts, rings, and balance-demanding movements. It is not 100 because seat height, elbow path, scapular position, and range of motion still matter.
- Progression: 90. It is strong for progressive overload because load is easy to add and repeat, especially on a selectorized or plate-loaded machine. It is not 100 because machine increments and machine-to-machine differences can be coarse.
- Support: 94. Back support, seated posture, and a guided path reduce balance and stabilization demands. It is not 100 because shoulder/scapular control still matters.
- Setup: 82. Setup is low-friction once the machine is available, but seat/handle adjustment and machine availability keep it below excellent.
- Hypertrophy: 86. It targets pectoralis major well for a press, and direct EMG work supports the pec/delt/triceps pattern. It is not isolation-level because anterior deltoid and triceps contribute meaningfully, and machine path/grip can shift emphasis.
- Fatigue efficiency: 84. It has a favorable stimulus-to-fatigue profile compared with unsupported free-weight pressing because balance, axial loading, and setup stress are low. It is not 100 because heavy pressing still creates shoulder, triceps, and local chest fatigue.
